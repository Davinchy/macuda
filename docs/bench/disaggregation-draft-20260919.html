Prompt processing on the RTX 5090. Generation on the Mac.

A model larger than the GPU's 32 GB of memory can still benefit from CUDA prompt processing. Experts stream from host memory over Thunderbolt during prefill, then the model's conversation state transfers to a Metal server for generation. These experiments measure the prefill stage and test the assumptions behind its cost model.

Measured prefill gains at longer context

measured, and partly supersededThe prefill speedup increases with prompt length. That direction has now been measured twice on Qwen3-Coder-Next and holds both times. The magnitude has not replicated. The first run put it at 2.35× → 5.55×; a re-measurement the following night, on the same model and machine, put it at 2.77× → 3.86×. The short end agrees; the long end does not, and the reason is on the Metal side. Both runs are below.

The earlier cost model assumed constant Metal prefill throughput of 660 tok/s, based on a measurement near 24,000 tokens. Both runs agree that it is not constant, and both agree closely at the short end — 673 and 641 tok/s. A constant-throughput assumption therefore understates the benefit at longer context in either case.

unresolved discrepancyThe card side replicated; the Mac side did not. The RTX 5090's times agree across the two runs to within 10% (15.0 → 13.5 s, 28.9 → 27.2 s), and Metal agrees at 24k (35.2 → 37.5 s). At 48k, Metal went from 160.3 s to 104.9 s — 53% faster on the second run, and that single figure is the entire difference between a published 5.55× and a measured 3.86×. Leading hypothesis, untested: during the first run the 49.6 GB model was read from an external SSD that was 100% full and measured at 41 MB/s; for the second it had been moved to a drive measured at 398 MB/s. A prefill long enough for the page cache to come under pressure would then pay disk reads in the first case and not the second. A same-night A/B on a card-resident model found no disk effect at all — but that case keeps every weight in VRAM after load, so it does not test this one. Until it is tested, the lower figure is the one to quote.
revised assumptionEarlier prediction: the speedup would fall from 4.2× at 24k tokens to 3.2× at 192k as the key-value (KV) cache displaced resident experts. The measurements above show the opposite direction within the tested range. They do not validate behaviour at 192k tokens, and the all-experts-on-host configuration does not directly test expert displacement.

The cost of crossing a microbatch boundary

llama.cpp processes a long prompt in microbatches, called ubatches. In this configuration, each ubatch contains at most 24,576 tokens and requires another transfer of the host-resident experts. The original six-point fit covered only single-ubatch prompts, so the repeated transfer cost had been inferred from source code without a measurement across the boundary.

measuredA measurable step at the boundary. Two prompts on either side of the boundary, each repeated twice with matching timings: 23,691 tokens (one ubatch) takes 15.0 s, and 26,156 tokens (two ubatches) takes 22.0 s. The fitted compute rate accounts for approximately 0.8 s of the increase. The remaining 6.2 s is consistent with another expert transfer.

Refitting the RTX 5090 cost model

measuredFour prefill measurements fit ⌈tokens ÷ 24,576⌉ × 6.21 s + tokens ÷ 3,300 + 1.65 s with a root-mean-square residual of 0.10 s. The revised estimates are: the expert stream costs 6.21 s per ubatch rather than 8.00, implying the link carries it at 7.0 GiB/s rather than 5.46; and a marginal prefill rate of 3,300 tok/s, replacing the earlier 5,150 tok/s estimate. These are fitted parameters for this model and configuration, not general hardware limits.

Model selection: compute and memory

Two model properties motivate this research: the amount of computation required per token and the amount of model data that must cross Thunderbolt.

Active parameters

In a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model, only a subset of experts is used for each token. Total parameter count therefore gives an incomplete picture of the work involved. The working hypothesis is that models with more active parameters will benefit more from CUDA prefill. The cross-model study is designed to test that relationship beyond the single model measured here.

Memory requirements

Weights, the KV cache and working buffers all consume memory. A model that fits entirely on the RTX 5090 can use the direct CUDA path. Split inference targets larger MoE models whose experts can be streamed during prefill and whose decode stage fits in the Mac's available memory. File size alone does not establish either fit.

design considerationExpert reuse differs between prefill and decode. A cache policy that benefits token-by-token generation may offer less benefit when processing a long prompt. The configuration measured here transfers every expert per ubatch; it does not assume a particular routing distribution.

Screening a model before downloading it

tools/disagg-screen.py reads a GGUF's header — a few megabytes at the front of the file, no weights mapped, no card involved — and estimates transfer volume, relative prefill time and a crossover prompt length. It does not score dense models larger than the GPU, because this workflow depends on expert streaming.

Two caveats on the tool, both found after it had already ranked candidates. It still uses the constant Metal-throughput assumption discussed above, so its long-context estimates are provisional. And until 2026-09-20 it read a sharded GGUF as its first shard alone — which is not a rounding error, since the transfer volume it understates is the fixed cost that sets the whole result: one 69 GB model screened as 37 GB streaming 7.3 GiB at 5.5×, where it is 69 GB streaming 39 GiB at 3.4×. Every model in this size band ships sharded. It also credited the card with its full 30 GiB for resident experts while ignoring the 8–11 GiB compute buffer a 24,576-token ubatch actually reserves; the resident budget is now 21 GiB. Both are fixed, but any ranking produced before that date was flattering.
